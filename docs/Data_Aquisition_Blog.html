Data Acquisition Blog

Introduction

I am a massive fan of fighting games, and I often find myself looking at online wikis that cover different characters’ strategies as well as their “frame data” (more on that later). I play multiple different fighting games (such as Street Fighter and Smash Bros.), but the series that I want to focus on for the sake of this project is called “Guilty Gear”. This series has gathered quite a large fan base since the release of its latest game, Guilty Gear -Strive-, and has seen thousands of fans register to play in several different tournaments.

However, some long-time Guilty Gear fans claim that the old games are better because Guilty Gear -Strive- has simplified many of the mechanics present in older Guilty Gear games. I thought I could try to approach this discussion by acquiring some data regarding the differences in “frame data” between the two games to see if any significant trends emerge between them

Context

For the sake of this blog, there are a few important things to understand about the attacks in this series.

  1. “Frame Data” refers to how many 1/60 second “frames” it takes for a certain attack to start, stop, etc. (this comes from the games running at 60 frames per second). For instance, if an attack has “20 frame startup”, that means a third of a second will pass between the attack button being pressed and the attack actually being able to hit the enemy.

  2. There are three significant attack properties discussed in the blog, which are startup (how fast an attack becomes active), recovery (effectively how long an attack’s cooldown is), and advantage on block (how much sooner you can act than your opponent can after hitting their block with an attack). These are all in terms of frames, though block advantage is positive or negative depending on whether you can act before your opponent exits “blockstun” or afterwards.

  3. Some attacks hit high, which means the the opponent must be standing and blocking to not get hit. The go-to defensive position is crouching and blocking, which blocks low attacks as well as mid attacks. As a result, high attacks are generally considered good options for catching your opponent off guard.

Questions

To gauge if Strive is more simplified than past Guilty Gear games, my goal is to look into how much attacks favor the attacker in terms of frame data between Guilty Gear Strive (Strive) and Guilty Gear Xrd Revelator 2 (Xrd). Key indicators that I will look out for include:

  • How fast attacks generally are to start up (faster attacks can make it easier to start your offense)
  • How fast High attacks tend to be (Faster highs are harder to react to)
  • How much recovery moves tend to have (more recovery means that enemies can punish missed attacks more easily)
  • How advantageous moves tend to be on block (having positive frame advantage on the opponent’s block means that it is harder for them to challenge your offense)

Data Scraping Ethics Check

The main webiste I used for my scraping was the Dustloop Wiki, which is a fanmade website that contains data and guides for various fighting games (Strive Page, Xrd Page). Before actually scraping anything, I made sure to check that its robots.txt didn’t forbid me from accessing anything, which it didn’t! I also made sure that there wasn’t anything in the Terms of Use that forbade me from accessing the site’s info.

Data Scraping Process

In order to get the data of characters’ attacks, I used the requests package as well as BeautifulSoup to make API requests and analyze the html. From there, I scraped the contents of the tables that contained the data of characters’ moves (denoted by class = "frameDataGridRow colCount-7" or class = "frameDataGridRow colCount-8"). I had to make some manual changes to the code that gathered those tables since a few characters’ data was stored differently (for instance, having colCount-8 tables where colCount-7 tables typically were), but my tables for the two different games were ready after that.

Lastly, I rearranged the structure of the tables such that the moves made up the rows rather than the characters, refit the rest of the data to reflect that, then finally cleaned up the data using RegEx to standardize the data entries.

EDA

The final dataset revealed many interesting insights about the differences in frame data between the games, such as:

  • Attacks in Xrd come out about 1.6 frames faster on average than in Strive (9.9 vs. 11.5). Additionally, the fastest moves in Xrd take 3 frames to come out, while the fastest moves in Strive take about 5 frames to come out.
  • High Attacks in Xrd come out nearly 2 frames slower on average than Highs in Strive (25.96 vs. 24.0), though they have significantly less recovery (15.42 vs. 25.18) and are less disadvantageous on block (-5.81 vs. -12.50).
  • Attacks in Xrd have about 3.3 fewer frames of recovery on average than those in Strive (14.21 vs. 17.55).
  • Attacks in Xrd are generally 2.55 frames more advantageous on block on average than attacks in Strive (-4.69 vs. -7.245).
  • 19% of the moves in Xrd are advantageous on block while about 8.37% of moves in Strive are advantageous on block. Interestingly, 83.78% of advantageous on block attacks in Strive correspond to the same attack type (a fighter’s Close Slash). This could be indicative of the developers trying to push a more standardized playstyle in Strive.

It seems that Xrd’s frame data is slightly generally stronger than Strive’s frame data. However, small differences in frame data can still have significant ramifications! Being two frames more advantageous on your opponent’s block can take you from being -1 (and likely losing to their fastest attacks) to being +1 (and conversely beating your opponent’s fastest attacks). Although the differences aren’t huge, I feel confident concluding that Strive is at least somewhat more streamlined than Xrd.
